## The problem

DISCO-2 is a Danish student satellite. Once it is in orbit, a group of
volunteers on the ground talks to it: telling it where to point the camera
on the next orbit, and everything else that has to be arranged with a
satellite.

That conversation happens through a terminal, and it happens on a
schedule. The satellite passes over the antenna and the window is about
ten minutes long. Someone has to be sitting there, ready, when it opens.

The volunteers we worked with were physics and mathematics students. Most
of the people who do this come from those subjects or from engineering,
so they can all program to some degree. They wanted to open the work to
volunteers who could not, and that is difficult when the only way in is a
terminal.

Underneath that sat a second problem. Volunteers stay for a semester or
two. A steep onboarding is expensive when the person you onboard leaves
after four months, and it means the people with experience are gone almost
as soon as they have it. What knowledge did get passed on was spread
across a lot of different platforms, which did not make it easier.

## My role

We were three on the project and worked closely together, with the tasks
shared rather than divided up. I was the group's point of contact with the
volunteers. I took part in the
weekly meetings where we framed the project and worked out which
functionality mattered, I ran the workshop, I sketched, I helped shape the
prototype, and I had the main responsibility for the final user test, which
became a large part of our conclusion.

The sketching turned out to matter more than I expected. I drew while we
talked, and the drawings did the work that words were doing badly. They
let the volunteers correct me quickly, and they gave my group members
something concrete to build from.

The workshop was inspired by the Future Workshop method, with a good
number of participants. That mattered. A method like that needs enough
people in the room to work at all.

## What we built

We wanted to see whether ideas from computational notebooks could be used
for the communication itself.

Instead of sitting ready at a terminal while the satellite crossed
overhead, the session could be prepared in advance. We took the kinds of
message they send most often, take a photo at these coordinates and so on,
and turned them into standard blocks. The blocks could be assembled into a
sequence, and the sequence would run at a chosen pass.

## What the final test told us

We had made it too simple.

The volunteers who tested it were programmers, and the blocks we had built
were more pedagogical than they wanted. One of them said he would rather
have something like block programming, where some of the logic is handled
for you but you are still programming.

Looking back, I think we lost sight of the volunteers who were already
there. Opening the work to people without a programming background was the
reason the project existed, and somewhere in building the prototype that
group took over our attention entirely. The volunteers the organisation
already had are the ones who keep the satellite running, and they are
likely to remain the majority. A design made for the people you hope to
attract is not worth much if it does not fit the people who are doing the
work today.

Both groups were real users. We simply stopped weighing them against each
other while we were building, and the test was where that showed.

## Outcome

The result was a prototype, not a finished system. It was handed over with
the bachelor report, and what the test showed became a large part of what
we concluded. I do not know what has happened with it since.

## Access to users is part of the work

Compared to other projects I have worked on, this one had something rare:
weekly contact with the people we were designing for, a workshop with
enough participants for the method to function, and a real test at the end
with time to act on it.

That access is not a detail. It decides whether design work is grounded in
anything, and it is the first thing I would try to secure on a new project.
